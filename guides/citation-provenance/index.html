Citation Provenance: Who Really Said That About You

By Chris Abraham · Published October 27, 2026

Ask an answer engine about me and the answer glows: three decades in the field, a shelf of publications, a reputation described in warm adjectives. Then open the citations. Roughly ninety five percent of the stellar material traces home to my own sites, my own essays, my own words wearing a dozen mastheads. I came online in 1993 and I wrote myself into existence as an expert, on purpose, over thirty years. This guide is my audit of what that record is worth now that the engines publish their sourcing.

The vacuum fills either way

Online perception management rests on a true premise. A name with no record is a vacuum, and vacuums fill with whatever the crawl finds first: a stale lawsuit, an angry review, a namesake in another state with a record of his own. Populate the record yourself and the engines answer from your material. Coverage answers questions before strangers ask them in worse venues. I proved the premise on the cheapest test subject available, me, and the result stands: ask the machines who I am and they recite the biography I spent three decades handing them. Any practitioner who calls the owned record worthless is selling absolution from work. The owned record is the foundation. The question this guide answers is what a foundation can hold.

The audit runs for free

AI search changed the terms by attaching the footnotes to the praise. Every answer carries its citation list, and the list is an audit trail anyone can run in minutes. Corroboration is the currency the engines trade in, and corroboration has grades. A claim repeated across five sites I own is one voice with five mouths. The engines read the registrations, the bylines, and the shared templates, and they grade self corroboration as the weakest form that still counts: a closed loop wearing the costume of consensus. Buyers learned the same trick. A partner, a journalist, or a procurement lead with ten minutes and a search box follows the footnotes home, and a record that all traces to the subject prices itself as a marketing asset. The praise still works on the incurious. Due diligence is a growth industry, and the incurious are a shrinking share of any room that signs contracts.

The hierarchy of receipts

Receipts come in three grades, and a serious program engineers toward the top of the list. Grade one is the self published record: your site, your essays, your profiles, your documentation. Necessary, load bearing, and limited. It proves the subject can write, publish, and sustain an argument across years. It answers every factual question a buyer brings. It cannot testify. A man grading his own homework earns high marks for penmanship.

Grade two is placed and syndicated repetition: the same claims carried on rented ground, contributed articles, sponsored roundups, syndicated profiles. This grade once passed for consensus. Engines now trace syndication back to its single origin, and repetition from one source decays toward grade one pricing. Treat grade two as scaffolding: useful while the real structure rises, worthless as the structure itself.

Grade three is independent staking: named testimonials from people whose own reputations ride on the sentence, referrals that cost the referrer something, coverage the subject had no hand in writing, and transaction backed marketplace history. My Upwork record is the plainest example I own: years of strangers paying real money and rating the work in public, on a platform whose whole business is keeping that ledger honest. A buyer trusts the ledger because the subject cannot reach inside it and revise. Independence is the entire product.

The practice standard

The standard I run for clients and for myself has two halves, and skipping either half fails the audit. First, build the owned record without embarrassment: complete, current, factual, deep enough that any question a buyer asks finds an answer in the subject's own hand. Second, earn the independent layer with the same seriousness: do work worth staking a name on, ask the named client for the named testimonial, keep the marketplace ledger spotless, and hand journalists material with receipts attached so their coverage stands without you in the room. Then measure the program the way an auditor would. Draw the citation graph behind your best answers and count how much of it the client does not own. That share is the reputation. Grow the share and the answers harden against any audit a competitor, a journalist, or a board can run. The owned record opens the door. Independent staking is what lets a stranger walk through it believing.
