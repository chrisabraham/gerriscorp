# Why reputation management only works when the behavior changes

By [Chris Abraham](https://gerriscorp.com/about/) · Published November 14, 2026

Online reputation management can change what a search for your name turns up. It can push bad results down, crowd them out with accurate material, and build a record that ranks above the damage. It cannot outrun the behavior that caused the problem. If the behavior continues, new complaints and fresh articles keep arriving, and each round of cleanup buys less than the round before it. I have argued in print since 2013 that the money only works after the client changes. This guide explains what the work can do, what it cannot do, and the order to do things in.

## What reputation management can do

Reputation management is defensive SEO: the same craft that ranks a page for a product, pointed at a name. Done properly, it finds everything a search turns up about you, answers what deserves an answer, corrects what is factually wrong at the source where that is possible, and publishes enough accurate material on properties you control to push the bad results down where almost nobody looks.

The building matters because silence is not neutral. I put it this way in a 2019 interview: "Google abhors a vacuum. When a community doesn't own its narrative, its narrative is vulnerable." If you publish nothing, the record of you is whatever other people happened to leave behind: an old lawsuit, an angry review, a namesake's arrest. Fill the vacuum and the picture changes: "If you create enough content, you can own the first two pages of Google."

Notice what is missing from that list: deletion. Honest criticism almost never comes down, and a demand letter rarely removes a true story. The realistic tools are displacement and dilution. You bury the bad result; you rarely delete it. It stays online, one needle in a growing stack of needles, too deep for a searcher to reach. A bad result sitting at position three is a crisis. The same result at position thirty is a footnote.

## What it cannot do

The work cannot make a false front true. As I said in the same interview: "Your reputation online can be curated, but it has to be the truth." A polished record built on behavior that has not changed is paint over a leak. My comparison for that is water: "Online reputation damage is like water damage. It'll seep through if you try to paper it over." The stain comes back because the pipe is still leaking, and the pipe is the conduct that produced the complaints in the first place.

It also cannot stop the flow. Every new bad review, every fresh article, every court filing is new material, and search engines prefer new material. A campaign can bury last month's story. It cannot bury next month's, because next month's has not been written yet.

## The law of diminishing returns

I worked this out in print in 2013, in an essay titled "Reputation management is not about dressing up mistakes." One of its sections is headed, without decoration, "The law of diminishing returns." The argument: brute force, backed by enough money, can hold a first page for a while, but Google normalizes the campaign, and "the newest, freshest, and most relevant real, organic, authentic, and popular content will bubble to the top." So the same monthly spend buys a smaller improvement every month, for as long as the underlying behavior keeps supplying that fresh content.

In the same essay I gave the two situations their names. If you have been done wrong, by error or by malice, the job is finite; I call that scenario "the clean." If your own conduct keeps producing the problem, the job has no end; I call that "repair and repeat." Of the second job I wrote: "Online reputation management does work, but it's an endless game of whack-a-mole."

The essay's opening still says the whole thing as plainly as I know how: "If you want to fix your online reputation, writing big checks to an online reputation agency isn't enough over the long haul—you'll also really need to get your own house in order."

## Own it first: mea maxima culpa marketing

When the fault is yours, the first move is not content. The first move is to own the mistake in public, specifically, and to make amends where amends can be made. I coined a name for the full-strength version in 2013: mea maxima culpa marketing. In the 2019 interview I described it in one breath: "It's mea maxima culpa marketing. You show you're flexible, you listen."

The apology has to be real, because a performed one backfires. From the 2013 essay: if the tone of contrition "seems hollow or false, you'll probably just make it worse, much worse." Amends are concrete: the refund issued, the job finished, the record corrected, the customer answered in the same place the complaint stands. That is not garnish on the campaign. It is the first piece of new evidence, and every later piece points back to it.

## The turnaround has a name

The change-first argument in this guide dates to my writing of 2013. The word I use for the turnaround itself is newer. It is metanoia, a Greek word for a change of mind deep enough to change direction: stop doing the thing, repair what can be repaired, and become the person the new record describes. A record built for someone who has not turned is a costume, and a costume costs more to maintain every year. A record built for someone who has turned is simply documentation.

## What you do first

If the problem is your own conduct, the order of operations is fixed, and the search work comes last:
1. Stop the behavior that keeps producing complaints. If customers keep reporting the same failure, the failure is a business process, not a run of bad luck.
2. Make amends where amends are possible: refunds issued, work finished, records corrected, and direct answers to the people affected.
3. Fix the process underneath, so the same failure stops recurring on a schedule.
4. Own what is already public: answer the serious criticism where it stands, once, with facts and with the change attached.
5. Only then start the search work in the next section.

One exception, and it matters. If the damage was done to you rather than by you—a false claim, a mistaken identity, a story that belongs to someone else—skip this section. That is the clean, a finite job with a finish line. As I wrote in 2013: "If you've simply been besmirched, write a check to me."

## How the work runs once the change is real
1. Audit the record. Search your name and your company the way a stranger, a customer, or a hiring manager would, and list what ranks on the first two pages.
2. Correct at the source. Factual errors get correction requests backed by evidence; outdated pages get updated or answered.
3. Respond where a response helps: once, in place, with the amends and the change stated plainly.
4. Build the record. Your own site, completed profiles, and steady publishing of accurate material fill the vacuum with the truth.
5. Tend it in small daily doses. Managing a reputation is like getting fitter: "Spend about 20 minutes a day, walk more, take the stairs at first. If you try too hard in a weekend, you might break something."
6. Monitor and repeat. Check the first two pages every month. Moving results is the braces; keeping them moved is the retainer, worn on a schedule. Because the behavior stopped feeding the problem, each month's gains hold instead of expiring, and the record compounds.
