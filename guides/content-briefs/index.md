# How to write a content brief for pages AI search will quote

By [Chris Abraham](https://gerriscorp.com/about/) · Published October 14, 2026

A content brief is a one-page instruction that settles a page's question, its answer, its evidence, and its limits before anyone writes a sentence. For AI search the brief carries more weight than it used to, because answer engines lift single passages out of a page and repeat them without the context around them. A page built from a good brief answers in its first lines, supports every claim, and stays inside its own question. I learned the discipline as an executive editor running a staff of writers, and it is the same job now with a new kind of reader.

## The question, word for word

Every brief starts with one question, written the way a buyer asks it. Take the wording from Search Console queries, sales call notes, or the questions in an AI visibility question set. "How do I fix Crawled, currently not indexed" is a brief. "Indexing issues" is a topic, and a page written from a topic answers nothing in particular.

Under the question, name who asks it and what they will decide after reading. A developer deciding whether a template is at fault needs a different page from a marketing director deciding whether to hire help, even when they type the same words.

## The answer, written by the expert first

The person who knows the subject writes the answer in two or three sentences before the writer starts. This is the passage an AI system is most likely to lift, so it has to stand alone: it names the thing, gives the answer, and includes the number or condition that makes it true. A writer can polish it. A writer working without it will produce a careful introduction that circles the answer for three paragraphs.

Google's guidance on helpful content asks whether a page provides original information and substantial value beyond what other pages offer. The expert's answer is where that originality lives: the threshold from your own projects, the failure you have seen five times, the step everyone skips.

## The evidence list

List every claim the page will make that a reader could doubt, and the source for each one: a vendor's documentation page, your own data with its date and sample, a public record. Writers open every source before citing it, and the brief says so. Answer engines cross-check, and a confident sentence with no support behind it is the sentence they drop or contradict.

Mark which evidence is first-party. Data from your own client work, anonymized, is the material no competitor can copy, and it is what turns a page into a source other pages cite.

## The sections, each one self-contained

Outline the sections as questions the reader will ask next, in the order they ask them. Each section opens with its own answer and makes sense if it is the only part quoted. Google says AI Overviews and AI Mode may run several related searches across subtopics to build a response, so a section that answers a sub-question cleanly can be the passage selected for that sub-search.

## The limits

A brief says what the page does not cover and where that material lives instead, with the link. This is how a site avoids two pages answering the same question, and it keeps each page short enough to finish. When the limit points to a page that doesn't exist yet, the brief has found the next brief.

## The links, title, and description

List the internal links the page must carry: up to the hub page for the subject, across to the pages a reader needs next, and from two or three existing pages that should now point to this one. Draft the title and meta description in the brief, inside the site's limits, because Google often uses the description for the snippet when it describes the page better than the page's own text. Structured data, where the page uses it, must match what the visible page says; Google's AI features guidance asks for exactly that.

## The review before publishing

Before a page goes live, someone other than the writer checks it against the brief: the answer is in the first lines, every claim has its source, the limits held, and every link resolves. AI drafting tools speed up the writing and change none of this. Google's guidance on generative AI content judges the result by its accuracy, quality, and relevance, and so does every reader who decides whether to trust you.
